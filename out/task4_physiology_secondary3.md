# Primary-Source Literature Verification: Host Physiology Parameters
## Salmonella enterica / Vibrio natriegens / Corynebacterium glutamicum

**Purpose:** Determine whether published, quantitative growth-law / resource-allocation data exist for these three hosts, to assess whether a "host physiological features" predictor set is buildable at all, and whether the underlying measurements are comparable across hosts (same medium/temperature class).

**Evidence tags:** [VERIFIED] = primary or clearly-attributed secondary source read directly, with citation. [INFERRED] = reasoning beyond what a source states (confidence noted). [NOT FOUND] = searched, nothing located; queries/sources tried are listed.

## Detailed Notes by Host

### Salmonella enterica
- The organism is the namesake of the classical Schaechter-Maaløe-Kjeldgaard growth law (1958) and of the Rosset-Julien-Monier (1966) RNA-composition-vs-growth-rate dataset — meaning the *foundational* bacterial growth-law datasets exist for this species, arguably even more so than for *E. coli* in the historical sense. However, both primary sources sit behind Microbiology Society / Elsevier paywalls that WebFetch could not penetrate (403 errors / no body returned), so the actual numeric tables (media list, doubling times, RNA% per medium) could NOT be verified in this pass — only their existence, journal, year, and qualitative conclusions.
- A modern, directly verified growth-rate value exists: μ ≈ 0.95 h⁻¹ / td = 44 min in M9 + 0.4% glucose, 37°C, strain LT2 (Mishra & Shashidhar 2022).
- RNA polymerase abundance: the one number that turns up in Salmonella-context searches (8400 molecules/cell) is explicitly an *E. coli* figure quoted inside a Salmonella paper for comparison — this is a common trap and must not be misattributed to Salmonella.
- Ribosomal proteome fraction (φ_R): no direct value found; the Klumpp/Scott/Pedersen/Hwa 2013 PNAS paper may be relevant but could not be accessed to confirm whether its Salmonella-labeled figures are actually measured in Salmonella or extrapolated/borrowed from E. coli (this paper is known in the field to work mostly with E. coli B/r and MG1655 — the "Salmonella" hit in search snippets needs primary-text confirmation before use).

### Vibrio natriegens
- Growth rate is the best-characterized metric, with values obtained from a paper (Hoffart et al. 2017) that explicitly cross-validates against Weinstock et al. 2016: doubling times of 9.4 min (Hoffart, BHIN complex medium, 37°C) vs. 9.8 min (Weinstock, complex medium, as cited by Hoffart). **The famous "~10 min" doubling time is confirmed as a real, reproducible, primary-sourced measurement**, not an urban legend — but it is specific to a rich complex medium (BHI + NaCl) at 37°C; in minimal glucose medium the rate drops to μ≈1.5–1.7 h⁻¹ (td ≈ 25–28 min), which is the condition that should be used if comparing to other hosts' minimal-medium numbers.
- Ribosome content (115,000/cell) traces to Aiyar, Gaal & Gourse 2002 (a well-cited, credible primary source, confirmed via two independent secondary citations), but the exact medium for that specific measurement could not be pinned down from the sources reachable here (login-walled at ASM journals) — flagged as [INFERRED, low-medium confidence] that it was standard LB/LBS+NaCl at 37°C, consistent with the rest of the Gourse-lab V. natriegens work, but not confirmed word-for-word.
- Proteome allocation to translation machinery (φ_R-equivalent) is the strongest, most modern, directly-measured data point of the entire report: 21.6–22.6% of proteome mass across a NaCl/growth-rate gradient in M9 minimal + glucose, 36.5°C (Mol Syst Biol 2023, PMC10090949, Table 2) — genuinely comparable in kind (mass-spec-based %proteome) to E. coli/C. glutamicum-style φ_R values used in growth-law literature.
- RNA polymerase abundance: NOT FOUND despite multiple targeted searches.

### Corynebacterium glutamicum
- By far the most complete, single-paper, growth-rate-resolved dataset of the three hosts is Nat. Commun. 2023 (14:5730, PMC10497606) — it reports ribosome abundance (absolute counts AND density), RNA/protein ratio, translation elongation rate, and active-ribosome fraction, ALL as continuous functions of growth rate (μ = 0.07–0.94 h⁻¹) under two comparable defined/rich media (CGXII minimal, BHI + glucose complex) at a consistent 30°C. This is the best candidate dataset for a quantitative growth-law comparator for this organism.
- A key qualitative finding directly relevant to cross-host comparability: C. glutamicum's growth-rate dependency of ribosome abundance and translation machinery **differs fundamentally from the classical E. coli/Salmonella-type linear growth law** — ribosome abundance is nearly flat below μ=0.4 h⁻¹ and the cell instead modulates translation elongation rate (5-fold range) and keeps a high active-ribosome fraction (>70%) at all growth rates. Any cross-host comparison that assumes a universal linear ribosome-vs-growth-rate law will misrepresent C. glutamicum.
- Maximum growth rate in defined (CGXII) minimal medium at 30°C is consistently reported in the 0.5–0.65 h⁻¹ range across three independent papers (Baumgart et al., Unthan et al. "Beyond growth rate 0.6," and the Nat. Commun. 2023 dataset) — this convergence across independent labs is a good comparability signal.
- RNA polymerase abundance: NOT FOUND.

## Cross-Host Comparability Assessment

1. **Temperature mismatch is a fundamental confound.** *S. enterica* and *V. natriegens* physiology data above were measured at **37°C**, while essentially all quantitative *C. glutamicum* growth-law data (ribosome content, R/P ratio, translation rate, φ_R) were measured at **30°C**, its physiological optimum. There is no 37°C growth-law dataset located for C. glutamicum, and no 30°C dataset for the other two. Any direct numeric comparison across all three hosts inherits this ~7°C temperature gap, which is known to shift ribosome content, growth rate, and translation rate substantially in bacteria generally — this should be treated as a first-order caveat, not a footnote.

2. **Medium type varies **within** each host's dataset** (minimal glucose vs. rich/complex), and the specific "best" dataset for each host uses a different medium philosophy: S. enterica's best-verified number is minimal (M9+glucose), V. natriegens's best growth-rate number is rich (BHIN) with a separate minimal-medium series available, and C. glutamicum's most complete dataset spans both CGXII (minimal) and BHI (rich) within the same paper — meaning C. glutamicum is actually the host where medium-matched, apples-to-apples comparison is most tractable internally, but only at 30°C.

3. **Data completeness is uneven and asymmetric across the 6 requested metrics.** V. natriegens and C. glutamicum both have strong, modern, mass-spectrometry- or SMLM-based φ_R/ribosome data (2023-era papers), while S. enterica's equivalent data is the oldest (1958, 1966) and was not numerically recoverable in this pass due to paywalls — meaning S. enterica is currently the weakest-evidenced host for metrics 2, 4, and 5 despite being historically the origin species for these concepts. RNA polymerase abundance (metric 3) is essentially **absent for all three hosts** as native, directly-measured values — the only number found (8400 molecules/cell, E. coli) is misattributable and must be excluded.

4. **Recommendation for benchmark runnability**: A defensible cross-host comparison could be built using growth rate (metric 1, well verified for all 3, though at different media/temp) and ribosome content / R/P ratio (metrics 2 and 4, well verified for V. natriegens and C. glutamicum, weakly verified for S. enterica pending paywall access). Proteome allocation to translation (metric 5) is strong for V. natriegens and structurally present but not numerically extracted for C. glutamicum, and effectively absent for S. enterica. RNA polymerase abundance (metric 3) should likely be **dropped from the comparison entirely** — it is not published as a native measurement for any of the three hosts.
